# Master data science: best practice for verifying entity resolution and MDM claims (research note C1, 2026-09-12)
## Summary
Entity resolution is a clustering problem, not just a pairwise classification. Good verification therefore needs pair level and cluster level evidence, plus proof that blocking did not hide missed matches. The strongest practice is to sample records or clusters with known inclusion probabilities, stratify clerical review by match weight, report precision and recall with intervals, and weight estimates back to the full population. Do not quote accuracy on imbalanced data, and do not estimate recall from only high score pairs. Use interpretable gates for thresholds, survivorship, blocking completeness, giant clusters, drift, and data quality dimensions. This note maps those practices to the fifteen deterministic gates already built, and ranks what to check first.

## 1. Measuring entity resolution quality
The core pair level outcomes are True Positive, False Positive, False Negative, True Negative, summarized in a confusion matrix [1]. From these, Splink defines Accuracy as (TP+TN) over all predictions, Recall as TP over (TP+FN), Specificity as TN over (TN+FP), Precision as TP over (TP+FP), and Negative Predictive Value as TN over (TN+FN) [1]. Composite measures include F beta as a weighted harmonic mean of precision and recall, with F1 giving equal weight, F2 weighting recall twice as important as precision, and F0.5 weighting precision twice as important, plus P4 and Matthews Correlation Coefficient that include non match performance [1]. Splink also defines predicted probability from match weight as 2 to the power M over 1 plus 2 to the power M [6].

Information retrieval and linkage literature warns that accuracy is deceptive when non matches dominate, which is the normal case. For n records there are order n squared non matches but only order n matches, so a naive classifier that predicts almost everything as non match can still show high accuracy [9]. Christen and Goiser show explicitly that any measure that includes True Negatives will be dominated by the huge TN count and will look optimistically high, and they recommend precision, recall, and F measure instead of accuracy and specificity for linkage quality [12].

Hand and Christen show a deeper problem with the F measure itself: it can be rewritten as a weighted sum of precision and recall where the weights depend on the linkage method being evaluated, so the relative importance of precision versus recall is set by the method rather than by the business problem [13]. That is a conceptual flaw if teams compare methods by a single F1 without stating costs.

Pairwise precision and recall are not enough for clustering quality. Binette et al. propose an entity centric framework that reports both pairwise and cluster level metrics, including pairwise and B cubed precision and recall and generalized merge distance, and that expresses global performance as weighted aggregates of cluster wise error metrics [9]. This matches the tool gates M10 for pairwise and B cubed precision on a gold cluster subset. The same work shows that naive computation of pairwise precision on a convenience benchmark is biased upward, often near 1, even when true population precision is much lower, and that combining biased precision with recall into F1 can reverse rankings of competing methods [9]. That finding supports M10 tolerance checks rather than exact equality.

Tilores summarizes the practical cost view that practitioners should carry into measurement: use F0.5 when false merges are dangerous, for example medical records, and F2 when missed matches are expensive, for example fraud screening, and choose the auto merge threshold accordingly [17]. Senzing auditing follows the same definitions, reporting Precision as Same Positives divided by Newer Count pairs, Recall as Same Positives divided by Prior Count pairs, and F1 as the harmonic mean, with score bands 0.98 to 1.0 excellent, 0.95 to 0.97 very good, 0.90 to 0.94 good but review, below 0.90 investigate [21].

## 2. Estimating precision and recall from a review sample
There are two defensible ways to estimate precision and recall, and both require sampling design, not just a labelled test set.

For pairwise estimates, the standard is stratified clerical review by linkage weight or score, because score strata are far more heterogeneous than random pairs. At Statistics Canada, reviews are done on a probability sample of record pairs, and when linkage is probabilistic the pairs are typically stratified according to their linkage weights [9]. The dissertation on stratified sampling of entity resolution results makes the same point: a systematic method with efficient clerical review across score strata is needed to assess accuracy without exhaustive review [16].

For cluster level estimates, Binette et al. recommend sampling records uniformly with replacement and recovering the full true cluster for each sampled record, which yields clusters sampled with probability proportional to size, that is p sub c equals cluster size over N [9]. Under that design, population quantities such as average cluster size and matching rate become ratios of expectations that can be estimated with weighted sample averages, and the same logic extends to error metrics such as overclustering and underclustering per record [9].

Two gate consequences follow directly. First, M7 requires that a precision claim be compared to a stratified clerical review estimate with an interval, not to a point estimate on a convenience benchmark. The literature supports this: Senzing audit shows the arithmetic for precision from Same Positives, but the Binette results explain why an unweighted benchmark precision is optimistic and needs weighting and an interval [9][21]. Second, M8 requires that a recall claim be supported by review strata below the merge threshold, with tolerance 0.05. This is necessary because a sample drawn only above the threshold cannot see False Negatives, which by definition are true matches that were scored below the threshold. The framework explicitly warns that sophisticated pair sampling schemes built around a single system do not transfer to other systems and do not yield representative estimates unless the sampling weights are used [9]. Tilores and Splink evaluation guides reinforce that threshold selection is a business decision that must be validated by spot checking on both sides of the threshold [2][17].

Where the pages give sampling mechanics, the concrete formulas matter. Definitions for cluster wise errors include Error Indicator 0 when predicted cluster equals true cluster else 1, Size Difference Error as predicted size minus true size, Overclustering Error as predicted minus true intersect size, and Underclustering Error as true minus predicted intersect size, then averaged within a cluster to get cluster level metrics [9]. Those definitions underpin the ranked recommendation to report both over and under clustering, not just a single error rate.

## 3. Blocking
Blocking controls scale by restricting comparisons to candidate pairs that share a blocking key, avoiding the n times n minus 1 over 2 full comparison space [4]. Splink presents blocking rules as SQL equi join conditions, noting that equi joins can be executed efficiently without materializing all pairs, while filter conditions such as levenshtein distance less than 3 force evaluation over all pairs then filtering, and that OR inside a single rule is extremely inefficient and should be replaced by multiple separate rules in a list [3][4][5].

The key quality trade off is pair completeness versus reduction ratio. Christen and Goiser frame blocking evaluation as characterizing both quality and complexity, and they note there is a tension between reducing comparisons and retaining true matches [12]. DeepWiki summarization of Splink states the same: loose rules mean more comparisons and better recall but slower runtime, strict rules mean fewer comparisons and potentially missed matches [5]. Splink offers count comparisons from blocking rule and analysis of largest blocks to diagnose skew [4][5].

M9 formalizes this: end to end recall cannot exceed blocking pair completeness, the fraction of true matches that ever enter the candidate pair set. This is a logical upper bound, not a tuning choice. If a claim reports recall of 0.92 but blocking analysis shows pair completeness of 0.85, the claim is impossible. The literature does not provide a universal blocking completeness threshold, teams must measure it on labelled clusters and report it. M11 extends the blocking story to cluster shape after transitive closure: a single weak or overly permissive blocking key can feed a large connected component that later explodes via closure.

## 4. Fellegi-Sunter and probabilistic linkage
The Fellegi Sunter model has three parameters: lambda the prior probability any two random records match, m the probability of an observed agreement pattern given the records are a match, and u the probability of the same pattern given they are not a match [6]. Informationally, m measures data quality and reliability, for example probability date of birth agrees given a true match, while u measures coincidence and cardinality, for example probability surname agrees given a non match [6]. A perfect unique identifier would have m equals 1 for agreement and u equals 0 [6].

Match weight is log base 2 of m over u for a feature, plus a prior term log base 2 of lambda over 1 minus lambda, and weights are additive across independent features [6]. Match probability is derived from match weight as 2 to the power M over 1 plus 2 to the power M [6]. Intuitively, match weight 0 maps to probability 0.5, 2 maps to 0.8, 3 maps to 0.9, 4 maps to 0.95, 7 maps to 0.99 [6]. Fellegi and Sunter 1969 is the foundational reference for this model (not among fetched pages).

Estimation practice in Splink uses probability two random records match estimated from deterministic rules with a guessed recall, for example block on first name and date of birth, then u estimated by random sampling of pairs with max pairs up to 1e8 or 1e9 for large data, and m estimated by expectation maximization, EM, blocked training where blocking fields are excluded from parameter estimation for that pass [8]. EM converges iteratively, for example largest change minus 0.521 in m for date of birth exact match in iteration 1 then diminishing changes [8]. This supports M12: for any field that is claimed to be discriminating, estimated m must exceed u for agreement levels, otherwise the feature provides negative evidence for a match and the match weight is negative. The pages do not prescribe a fixed numeric gap, only the directional inequality plus additivity under independence.

## 5. Over-merging and cluster shape
Transitive closure is the logical inference that if A matches B and B matches C then A matches C, implemented by finding connected components in a graph where nodes are records and edges are match decisions [53][54]. This is powerful for building canonical clusters but dangerous: a single false positive edge can merge two otherwise distinct entities and then propagate, a phenomenon often called chaining [53][54].

Splink and incremental ER literature does not give a universal giant cluster size, the gate M11 threshold size greater or equal to max of 50 and 1 percent of records is therefore an operational convention rather than a derived optimum. The justification is twofold. First, empirical entity size distributions are typically small, for example inventor disambiguation with millions of entities each with a handful of patents [9], so a component containing 1 percent of an entire dataset is extreme and almost certainly indicates over merging or a bad blocking key. Second, dynamic maintenance literature shows that decremental connectivity after deletes or match corrections is expensive and can require re checking connectivity across a whole component, so large components create both quality and engineering risk [53]. The DB research note also gives a worked bridge record example where inserting one bridge merges two components and deleting it splits them again, illustrating why incremental systems need split detection [53].

Binette et al. provide monitoring statistics that help detect over merging early: average cluster size, matching rate proportion of records in clusters larger than 1, and Hill numbers for the cluster size distribution, plus homonymy and name variation rates for label noise [9]. For error analysis they define record wise Overclustering Error and Underclustering Error and their cluster averages, which together make the impact of a giant cluster concrete: a single giant component drives OCE high for many records [9].

## 6. Golden records and survivorship
A golden record is the single best curated version of an entity after deduplication and merge, built from surviving attribute values across contributing records [54][34]. Survivorship is not one rule but a sequenced rule set evaluated top to bottom, with first wins or most trusted wins semantics depending on the platform [33][32].

The pages list the recurring patterns. Stibo Systems documents trusted source rules where lower trust sources never overwrite higher trust, most recent rules where the newest revision or Last Edited date wins, and the need to order rules explicitly [33]. Oracle Customer Data Management provides six predefined set attribute value rules, including Most Recently Updated Organization or Person Attribute where most recent date wins and Highest Source Confidence Level Wins where highest source confidence wins [32]. The Informatica MDM hands on guide describes tokenization, trust and survivorship with concrete trust levels such as CRM 90, ERP 80, Excel 50, and fuzzy match thresholds usually 0.7 to 0.9 [30]. LumenData on Informatica SaaS adds merge outcomes auto merged, queued for manual merge, or skip, and notes threshold based merging only with fuzzy match [31]. Data Ladder generalizes survivorship into six approaches: trusted source wins, most complete wins, most recent wins, verified value wins, most frequent wins, and conditional overwrite when destination is empty [34].

M2 and M15 capture the engineering lesson: if survivorship rules are not declared per attribute, audits cannot decide whether a surviving value came from the intended source, recency, completeness, or frequency logic, and lineage cannot be reconstructed. The best practice is to declare the rule, the source system ranking, the conflict rate per attribute, and whether nulls are allowed to overwrite, then validate merged samples as shown in Informatica SaaS examples [31][34]. There is no universal correct trust ranking, it is a business policy, but it must be explicit and versioned.

## 7. Data quality dimensions and how leading tools express them
Two complementary standards anchor this area. ISO 8000 is about how to verify and exchange quality data, with three measurable dimensions in ISO 8000-8: syntactic quality verified against a declared syntax, semantic quality verified against an open technical dictionary, and pragmatic quality validated by user fitness for purpose [35][36]. ISO IEC 25012, via ISO IEC 25024, defines what quality characteristics exist, typically listed as 15 including accuracy, completeness, consistency, credibility, currentness, accessibility, compliance, confidentiality, efficiency, precision, traceability, understandability, availability, portability, and recoverability, split into inherent, system dependent, and both [37]. DAMA DMBOK is described as a broader knowledge framework, not a normative standard, while ISO 8000 is normative and auditable with pass or fail conformance [36].

For monitoring, the pages converge on six core DAMA style dimensions most used in practice: accuracy does the data reflect real truth, completeness is expected data present, consistency does the same fact match across systems, timeliness is data current enough, validity does data conform to format and range, uniqueness are there no unwanted duplicates [38][39][40]. Each has a natural SQL check, for example completeness as missing count, validity as regex or range checks, uniqueness as duplicate count, timeliness as max inserted at lag [40].

Leading tools map directly to those dimensions. Great Expectations provides declarative Expectations such as expect column values to not be null for completeness, expect column values to be unique for uniqueness, expect column values to be between and regex match for validity, and expect column values to be in set plus cross table profiling for integrity [41][42][43]. Soda Core expresses the same checks in SodaCL with metrics missing count, missing percentage, duplicate count, duplicate percent, invalid count, row count, freshness, and schema checks, plus warn and fail alert levels and numeric distribution metrics such as avg, stddev, percentile [44][45][46]. Deequ on Spark formalizes data unit tests with a VerificationSuite, Check, Constraint, and Analyzer model, optimizing with scan sharing and optional metrics repositories, plus DQDL declarative rules such as IsComplete, IsUnique, Completeness greater than, and row level result DataFrames that add a boolean column per check [47][48][49]. dbt adds lightweight but ubiquitous tests unique, not null, accepted values, and relationships for referential integrity, plus singular SQL tests that return failing rows, with explicit notes like unique also fails on nulls so pair with not null, and relationships excludes nulls by design [58][59][60].

M14 requires that claims map each quality dimension to an explicit threshold and method, and critically that accuracy claims name a reference method. This follows directly from the accuracy definition: without a trusted reference such as cross reference to source system, sampling and manual review, or a master reference, accuracy cannot be measured automatically [40]. The pages treat thresholds as business decisions constrained by measurement cost.

## 8. Drift and re-labelling
Population Stability Index, PSI, is sum over bins of (actual percent minus expected percent) times ln of actual percent over expected percent, computed on the same bins applied to both samples, often deciles derived from the baseline [51][52]. DarwintIQ applies the same reading to trading models by comparing the recent return distribution to a baseline, noting PSI below 0.1 indicates stable distributions, 0.1 to 0.25 moderate shift worth investigating, and above 0.25 significant drift [50]. GeeksforGeeks and Runcell repeat identical bands: PSI less than 0.1 no significant change continue monitoring, 0.1 to 0.25 moderate shift investigate driving bins, greater or equal 0.25 significant change recalibrate or redevelop [51][52].

For gate M13, two facts are essential. First, the bands 0.10 and 0.25 are industry convention from credit scorecard monitoring, not critical values of a statistical test, and they should be read alongside other distance metrics and economic impact [50][52]. Second, PSI is symmetric when populations are swapped because both factors change sign together, and it is sensitive across all bins, which makes it good for broad drift rather than a center focused test [50][52]. Runcell explicitly warns that thresholds are not a statistical test, that high stakes models may use tighter bars, and that large samples can push PSI above 0.1 on an economically meaningless shift [52]. In MDM terms, PSI should be computed on match scores or on selected input features, often called Characteristic Stability Index when applied to a feature, and a rising PSI should trigger investigation of regime change, acquisition channel change, or policy cutoff change before performance visibly degrades [50][52].

## 9. What leading products do (Splink, Zingg, Senzing, Tamr, Reltio, Informatica, Great Expectations, Soda, Deequ, Monte Carlo, dbt, and any other product the pages cover)
Splink is a probabilistic Fellegi Sunter engine with explicit blocking, EM estimation, match weights and waterfall charts for per comparison evidence [6][8]. It documents edge metrics from ground truth, threshold selection tools, blocking performance analysis, and term frequency adjustments [1][2][3][4][5]. It expects labelled data for edge evaluation and accuracy analysis from labels column or table [1].

Zingg is an ML based master data and entity resolution system that learns a blocking or clustering model plus a similarity model, explicitly to reduce the quadratic comparison space, reporting typical comparisons as 0.05 to 1 percent of the full space, with interactive training data building and support for multiple languages [18]. Documentation depth for quality metrics is lighter in the fetched pages, which focus on architecture and blocking value [19][20].

Senzing provides an entity resolution engine plus exploratory data analysis tools sz explorer, sz snapshot, and sz audit [22]. sz audit compares results against a truth set and reports precision, recall, and F1 with detailed audit summary tables, Statistics and Pairs and Accuracy columns, and MERGE versus SPLIT review categories that route ambiguous matches to why and how commands for explainability [21]. This is the most direct product implementation of the expected audit gate.

Tamr is an AI native mastering platform that emphasizes machine learning based entity resolution, mastering with human feedback, real time search before create to prevent duplicates re entering, and enrichment with referential matching [24][25][26]. Public pages describe discovery of duplicates and flagging of quality issues such as missing or invalid values, formatting issues, and the broader data quality workflow, but do not publish fixed precision or recall thresholds [24][25].

Reltio is a cloud MDM focused on match rule design and tuning. It recommends testing on a statistically meaningful sample, for example 10 percent of total volume with a floor of 10M records, loading without match rules first to validate profiles, authoring rules in JSON or the Data Modeler with Rule Analyzer, rebuilding match tables, and iteratively reviewing potential matches for false positives versus missed matches until business objectives are met [27][28]. Support pages reinforce match tuning as an iterative, cost versus false positive trade off rather than a single acceptance number [29].

Informatica MDM implements Match and Merge with tokenization, trust and survivorship, match types exact, fuzzy, and probabilistic, and merge outcomes auto merge versus manual merge queued for steward review, with standard trust rankings and typical fuzzy thresholds 0.7 to 0.9 [30][31]. Stibo, Oracle, and Data Ladder pages show the same survivorship families described earlier, reinforcing that golden record quality depends on declared rules and lineage [32][33][34].

Great Expectations, Soda, Deequ, and dbt are not entity resolution matchers but data quality verification layers that can verify the inputs and outputs of resolution. Great Expectations offers 300 plus Expectations and suites plus Data Docs and checkpoints for CI [41][43]. Soda offers SodaCL checks for row count, missing count, duplicate count, invalid count, freshness, distribution, schema, and cross table reference checks with Soda Cloud history for change over time thresholds [44][45][46]. Deequ offers unit tests for data on Spark with VerificationSuite, analyzers, and DQDL plus metric staging for anomaly detection [47][48][49]. dbt offers generic tests unique, not null, accepted values, relationships that run as select for failing rows [58][59][60].

Monte Carlo is referenced in the prompt but does not appear among the fetched pages, so no product specific claims are cited for it here. No fetched page provides quantitative benchmark comparisons across products, so comparative accuracy claims cannot be cited.

## Recommendations
1. **Sample for both precision and recall, not just precision.** WHY: Naive benchmark precision is optimistic and can be near 1 even when true population precision is lower, and rank reversals appear when biased precision is combined into F1 [9]. Binette et al. show representative estimates require probability sampling with weights back to the population [9]. Maps to **M7, M8**. Cost of not doing it: teams ship a high F1 that hides over merging, then discover customer duplication in production.

2. **Stratify clerical review by match score and always include below threshold strata.** WHY: At Statistics Canada reviews are stratified by linkage weights and can be double reviewed for quality control, and the stratified design is what makes recall estimable [9]. A sample drawn only above the merge threshold cannot estimate recall because False Negatives live below [9]. Maps to **M8 contributes 0.05 tolerance, M7 interval**. Cost of not doing it: recall is overstated, blocked true matches are invisible, downstream analytics silently miss entities.

3. **Report blocking pair completeness and enforce end to end recall upper bound.** WHY: Splink blocking analysis shows how rules trade completeness against comparisons, and Christen and Goiser frame quality versus complexity with reduction ratio [3][5][12]. End to end recall cannot exceed the fraction of true matches that survived blocking [12]. Maps to **M9**. Cost of not doing it: impressive match thresholds still miss matches that were never scored.

4. **Hold match rate and accuracy together, and distrust accuracy alone on imbalanced data.** WHY: Accuracy equals (TP+TN) over all predictions and is dominated by the huge TN count in linkage, so even classifying everything as non match can look accurate [1][12]. M6 requires sampled accuracy to accompany match rate for this reason. Maps to **M6**. Cost of not doing it: dashboards show 99 percent accuracy while true matching is poor.

5. **Require m greater than u for agreement, and show learned m and u.** WHY: In Fellegi Sunter, m is agreement given match and u is agreement given non match, and the Bayes factor m over u determines the additive match weight [6]. If m is not greater than u for an agreement level, the feature penalizes rather than supports a match. EM and random sampling estimation make these numbers inspectable [8]. Maps to **M12**. Cost of not doing it: a field that looks useful actually harms linking and thresholds become uninterpretable.

6. **Enforce threshold bands, review lower bound less or equal merge, and tie to costs.** WHY: Fellegi Sunter decision rule uses thresholds t lower and t upper to map R to match, possible, and non match, and modern practice routes middle band to human review [12][17]. Tilores explicitly maps F0.5 to high precision medical merging and F2 to high recall deduplication [17]. Maps to **M1**. Cost of not doing it: threshold semantics drift, auto merge becomes either too timid or too aggressive for the use case.

7. **Automatically review giant clusters.** WHY: Transitive closure via connected components means a single false edge can merge an entire component, and incremental literature treats giant clusters as both quality and engineering risk including expensive split detection on deletes [53][54]. No page gives a derived size, so the gate threshold max of 50 and 1 percent is a convention. Maps to **M11**. Cost of not doing it: one bad blocking key creates a 10k record cluster that contaminates analytics and is hard to unwind.

8. **Report pairwise and B cubed precision on a gold cluster sample with weighting.** WHY: Binette et al. define an entity centric framework that aggregates cluster wise errors to global pairwise and B cubed metrics and that warns that unweighted benchmark computation is biased [9]. This aligns with Christen and Goiser need for record pair and classification measurement [12]. Maps to **M10 tolerance 0.05**. Cost of not doing it: pair metrics hide entity level fragmentation and comparison across systems is meaningless.

9. **Monitor distribution drift on scores and features with PSI and declare convention.** WHY: PSI formula with bins and bands less than 0.1 stable, 0.1 to 0.25 moderate, greater or equal 0.25 significant is consistent across trading and credit risk sources [50][51][52]. Runcell warns these are conventions not statistical tests, sensitive to sample size and bin count, and may need tighter bars for high stakes [52]. Maps to **M13 PSI bands 0.10 and 0.25**. Cost of not doing it: model decays silently after a channel or population shift until downstream KPIs move.

10. **Declare survivorship rules per attribute and keep lineage.** WHY: Stibo requires ordered survivorship rules and trusted source ordering, Oracle ships predefined most recent and highest confidence rules, and Data Ladder systematizes trusted, most complete, most recent, verified, most frequent, and conditional overwrite [32][33][34]. Without per attribute declaration, audit cannot decide if a survived value is policy correct. Maps to **M2, M15 with conflict rate per attribute**. Cost of not doing it: golden records are irreproducible, stewards disagree, and compliance cannot trace why a field survived.

11. **Pin a data quality contract to ISO and DAMA dimensions with measurable checks.** WHY: ISO 8000 defines syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic quality that can be verified against specifications [35][36], while ISO IEC 25012 and DAMA give the characteristic sets [37][38]. Tools provide direct implementations: Great Expectations Expectations for null, unique, range, regex, and referential integrity [42][43], Soda metrics missing count, duplicate count, invalid count, freshness, schema [44][45], Deequ VerificationSuite with IsComplete, IsUnique, Completeness [47][48], dbt tests unique, not null, accepted values, relationships [58][59]. Maps to **M14 accuracy needs reference method**. Cost of not doing it: syntactic checks pass while semantic gaps, for example long truncation, still fail the use case.

12. **Treat F measure as a summary, not a decision rule, and state beta.** WHY: Hand and Christen derive that F measure implicitly weights precision and recall by the method itself, which violates the principle that the trade off should be set by the problem owner [13]. Choose beta explicitly to reflect false merge versus missed merge costs [13][17]. Maps to **new gate: cost weighted metric declaration**. Cost of not doing it: teams optimize the wrong harmonic mean and then misreport business risk.

13. **Size and stratify review using power thinking, and report residual error.** WHY: Binette et al. explicitly discuss sampling with probability proportional to size offering smaller standard errors when errors correlate with cluster size, and give ratio of expectations estimators for summary statistics [9]. Pullen describes the cost of systematic clerical review at scale [16]. This implies review sample size and residual error after review are first class gates. Maps to **M4, M5 sample size and residual error**. Cost of not doing it: estimates are statistically fragile, confidence intervals are wide, and false merge cost class is meaningless.
